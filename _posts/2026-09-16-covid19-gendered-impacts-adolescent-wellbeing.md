The immediate health toll of COVID-19 was measured continuously. The effect on adolescents living through school closures, lost household income, and months of confinement was much harder to see, particularly in settings where existing surveys were not designed to capture it.

A paper in *eClinicalMedicine* looks at that question across three very different contexts. The citation is Oakley, Abuhamad, Seager, Avuwadah, Hamory, Jones, Malachowska, Yadete, Abu Hamad, and Baird (2022), *eClinicalMedicine* 52.

## The design

The study draws on phone surveys with 5,752 adolescents in Ethiopia, Jordan, and Palestine, collected between November 2020 and February 2021. Two features are worth noting.

First, the outcome measures were locally adapted rather than imported wholesale. Wellbeing constructs that travel poorly across cultures were adjusted for each setting, which matters when the comparison spans rural Ethiopia and urban Jordan.

Second, the analysis centers on two household-level variables: vulnerability to COVID-19 related shocks, such as income loss or illness in the household, and receipt of social protection in the form of cash transfers or food aid. The question is how adolescent wellbeing varies with the first, and whether the second offsets it.

This is a cross-sectional study, so the results describe associations rather than causal effects. That framing matters for how much weight the policy conclusions can carry.

## What the study found

Adolescents in more COVID-vulnerable households reported lower coping resilience and heavier domestic and care responsibilities. The burden was not shared evenly. Girls reported over two additional hours of domestic and care work compared with boys, and girls in vulnerable households also faced tighter restrictions on their mobility and behavior.

The social protection finding is the uncomfortable one. Receiving cash transfers or food aid showed no direct association with better adolescent wellbeing, and only partially buffered the relationship between vulnerability and poor outcomes among *less* vulnerable households. The households in the worst position were the ones the transfers helped least.

Disability, having dropped out of school, and being married were each associated with worse outcomes, and those disadvantages tended to compound rather than sit in isolation.

## Why it matters

Two things stand out.

The first is that a shock which looks gender neutral on paper is not gender neutral in the household. School closures did not simply pause education for everyone equally. They reallocated unpaid labor, and that reallocation fell on girls. Any accounting of pandemic costs that stops at learning loss will understate what happened to adolescent girls.

The second is about instrument design. Social protection systems in these three settings were built to move resources to households in ordinary times. Judged by whether they protected adolescent wellbeing during a compound shock, they did not do much, and they did least for the households furthest down. Transfers sized for a normal year do not automatically scale to a crisis, and household-level targeting does not guarantee that anything reaches the adolescents inside the household.
